## The Wall Street Golden Era: 1990s Trading

Let me paint you a picture of the 1990s. Picture this: a crowded trading floor at Goldman Sachs <HighlightedText>Goldman Sachs</HighlightedText>, screaming brokers with multiple phone lines, handwritten order tickets, and the *smell* of coffee and stress.

In the 90s, if you wanted to trade stocks, you had to:
- Call your broker (yes, actually speak to a human)
- Pay $50-100 per trade in commissions
- Wait for a fax confirmation (a FAX!)
- Have access to real-time quotes that were minutes delayed
- Be either wealthy, institutional, or incredibly determined

The beauty of this era? Traders actually *understood* what they were doing. Information moved slowly. You had time to think. The biggest traders made money through genuine research, relationship management, and old-fashioned hustle.

<Callout type="info" title="Did You Know?">
In 1995, the average stock trade took 3 business days to settle. Routers didn't exist. Internet speed was measured in kilobits. A trader who could execute 5 trades in a day was considered active.
</Callout>

## The 2000s: The Birth of Speed

Fast forward to the early 2000s. The internet boom happened. Suddenly, retail traders like me could actually participate. E-Trade and TD Ameritrade decimated the commission model. I could now trade for $5-10 per trade from my home.

But here's where things got weird: **speed became everything**.

The 2000s saw the rise of day trading culture. People quit their jobs to trade. Some got rich (and documented it on forums). Most lost money. Commissions were lower, but volatility was higher. And somewhere in the shadows, institutional traders started building the first algorithmic systems.

In 2008, all hell broke loose.

## The Financial Crisis of 2008: When Speed Became a Weapon

I wasn't trading in 2008, but I watched from my college dorm as markets collapsed. On October 19, 2008—"Black Monday II"—the Dow fell nearly 7%. That same day, it recovered. Trading volumes hit historic highs. People were panicked, and algorithms were... confused.

Here's what most people don't understand about 2008:

The market didn't crash purely because of housing debt. It crashed because trading systems—both human and algorithmic—all wanted to sell at the same time. Automated portfolio insurance programs kicked in. Margin calls forced liquidations. This created a cascading effect that looked like a digital avalanche.

By 2009, markets had bottomed, and recovery began. But the lesson was clear: algorithms were here to stay, and they were getting smarter.

## The 2010s: My Trading Era

This is when I entered the game (badly). The 2010s were wild because:

- Commissions dropped to near zero (Robinhood launched in 2013)
- Retail traders could now use mobile apps
- Information became instant (social media, financial Twitter, Reddit)
- Stock ownership became "trendy" for Gen Z

I made every mistake in the book. <HighlightedText>FOMO(HFT)</HighlightedText>-bought Bitcoin at $18,000. Panic-sold Apple at $150 (it's now $235). But I also started learning. I watched YouTube videos from traders I respected. I lost money on NVIDIA when it was "boring." I made money on COVID stocks before dumping them.

The thing about the 2010s? Retail traders could still compete because human psychology was still the main driver. News came out → people reacted → the market moved. You could read the room, catch trends, and make decent returns.

### The Algorithm Rises (Silently)

But underneath the surface, something crucial was happening: **institutional traders and tech companies were building AI-powered systems that could analyze millions of data points in microseconds.**

By 2015, <HighlightedText>high-frequency trading (HFT)</HighlightedText> accounted for nearly 50% of US equity volume. These algorithms didn't think. They didn't research. They exploited inefficiencies and human psychology at speeds humans couldn't even perceive.

## Global Markets in 2025: The Kenyan Perspective

Since I'm based in Nairobi, I want to address something important: **global markets have never been more connected, and yet more unequal**.

The <HighlightedText>Nairobi Securities Exchange (NSE)</HighlightedText> has stocks like Safaricom, Equity Bank, and KCB Group. These are solid companies with real cash flows. But trading volume is a fraction of what it is on Nasdaq. On any given day, trading on the NSE might be $5-10 million. Compare that to NVIDIA alone: $30-50 billion daily.

This creates an arbitrage opportunity and a tragedy simultaneously:
- Opportunity: Kenyan stocks trading at reasonable valuations with less attention from algorithms
- Tragedy: Most international capital flows go to the "bigger" markets, leaving African markets undercapitalized

My approach? I allocate 70% to global markets (NVIDIA, Tesla, etc.) and 30% to NSE stocks. The NSE offers better fundamentals and less algorithmic interference, but lower liquidity.

## The Real Mystery: Are Markets Still Beatable?

Here's what keeps me up at night: **In 1990, a smart, hardworking trader could consistently beat the market. In 2025, can they?**

The evidence suggests no. Here's why:

- **Market efficiency**: With millions of algorithms analyzing data simultaneously, arbitrage opportunities disappear in milliseconds
- **Information availability**: Everyone sees the same news at the same time
- **Passive investing dominance**: Trillions are in index funds now, which creates less inefficiency to exploit
- **Regulatory complexity**: Trading is regulated to death (good), but this removes easy exploits

Yet, somehow, I still make money. How? I think it's because:

1. I focus on long-term trends, not short-term noise
2. I use bots for the boring stuff and my brain for what matters
3. I don't fight against algorithms; I align with them
4. I stay emotionally disciplined (hard, but critical)

<Callout type="info" title="The Uncomfortable Truth">
Most returns in 2025 come not from beating the market, but from:
- Time in market (compounding)
- Picking uncrowded sectors before they blow up (NVIDIA, AI stocks)
- Using leverage wisely (bots with risk management)
- Simply not panicking during crashes
</Callout>

## Key Takeaways

Here's what I've learned after years of trading and watching this industry evolve:

**1. Speed isn't always better.** The traders who made the most money in the 2000s weren't day traders—they were patient investors. The traders making the most money in 2025 are letting bots execute precise strategies they've tested, not trying to outsmart every market move.

**2. Understand the tools, but don't be enslaved by them.** A bot is just executing what you tell it. If your logic is bad, no amount of automation helps. I've seen people blame Pionex for losses when their strategy was fundamentally flawed.

**3. Global markets are accessible, but also stacked.** You can trade NVIDIA from Nairobi in 30 seconds. But you're competing against algorithms designed by PhDs at hedge funds. Focus on your edge, not on pure volume.

**4. 2008 was a warning that still applies.** When everyone panics, systems amplify panic. Having cash reserves and emotional discipline beat any bot in a crash.

**5. The best returns come from boring strategies.** Buying Tesla or NVIDIA and holding wasn't exciting. But it worked. My best trades were the ones I made while bored at 2 AM and then forgot about.

The trading mystery isn't really about markets. It's about humans trying to stay relevant in a world increasingly run by algorithms. In 1990, a trader's value came from knowledge. In 2000, from speed. In 2025, from wisdom.

Because at the end of the day, bots are great. They don't panic. They execute perfectly. But they can't adapt to the weird, unprecedented situations that happen in markets. That's where humans still win.

And that's why I'm still here. Not because I can beat the algorithm—I can't. But because I can choose which game to play, and that's an edge no bot will ever have.
